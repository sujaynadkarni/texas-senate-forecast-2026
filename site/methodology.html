How the forecast works

1. Start from the polls

We average every head-to-head poll from the last 60 days. Recent polls count more (a poll loses half its weight every 14 days), larger polls count a little more, and polls paid for by a party or campaign count less. If one pollster releases several polls, those polls share their weight so a single firm cannot dominate.

2. Measure how wrong polls can be

Texas polls have picked the winner in every recent race, but the final margin has been off by 2 to 5 points, and not always in the same direction. We let the true result differ from the polls by about that much, with a small chance of a bigger miss.

3. Spread the statewide number across 254 counties

Each county's lean relative to the state comes from past races for the same office: the 2018 and 2024 Senate races for Senate, and the 2018 and 2022 governor races for governor. Counties are grouped into six regions (big cities, big suburbs, outer suburbs, South and West Texas, mid-size cities, rural) that tend to move together, so a region can swing more or less than the state. 2026 primary turnout nudges each region slightly.

4. Estimate turnout

Each county's expected turnout is its 2018 turnout rate (the last high-energy midterm) applied to 2026 registration, with random variation by county, region and state.

5. Run 50,000 elections

Each simulated election draws a polling miss, regional swings, county noise and turnout, then adds up every county's votes. A candidate's chance of winning is the share of the 50,000 simulations they win. "74%" means the candidate wins about 3 times in 4, not that they get 74% of the vote.

6. Check it against the past

Run as if it were 2018 and 2024, using only data available then, the model favored the eventual winner both times, the actual margin fell inside its 80% range both times, and it picked the right winner in 251 of 254 counties in 2018 and 243 of 254 in 2024. Two backtests are not proof, but they show the model is not badly off.

Election night

Before votes are counted, the needle shows the forecast. On Nov. 3 it updates every 10 minutes from the state's county-by-county results. Each simulated election is scored against the votes counted so far; simulations that match the count count more, and the needle moves to the share of matching simulations each candidate wins.

Mail ballots and early votes are usually counted first and lean Democratic in Texas cities. We expect that and correct for it, so an early lead in the first results does not move the needle more than it should.

Early voting

  • Turnout, not votes. Counties and the state report how many people voted, never whom they voted for. Candidate totals stay sealed until polls close on Nov. 3.
  • Live today uses each county's own website during the day. A county appears only once it has posted numbers for today. These counts are unofficial; the state's numbers replace them once posted.
  • Comparisons are raw vote counts against the same day of early voting in past years: "142% of 2022 day 1" means 42% more in-person votes so far today than in that county's whole first day in 2022.
  • The map filter shows today's in-person votes, mail ballots, or both together, each against the same point in 2022, using the same numbers as Live today.
  • Who's turning out groups every county by the 2024 presidential result and compares its early vote with the same point in 2022. Counties whose numbers are clearly incomplete are left out on both sides.
  • Turnout effect applies each county's turnout pace (relative to the state's) to our forecast of how that county votes, and reports how much that would move the margin. Counties not posted yet keep the statewide pace. It is shown separately and never changes the forecast or the needle.
  • Early voters by 2026 primary matches early voters against people who voted early or by mail in the March 2026 primaries. Primary-day voters are not in the state's free lists, and the 2026 Democratic primary had more early voters than the Republican one, which lifts the Democratic share. Primary voters are a clue to party, not votes.
  • Key counties are grouped by the role they play: Democratic vote banks, battlegrounds, and Republican vote banks.

Assumptions and limits

  • Mail ballots in Harris County ran about 30 points bluer than the county in 2024. Outside Harris we assume a smaller lean (about 12 points on the margin); we have no county records to check it.
  • There are no published 2018 poll averages for these races, so the 2018 rows in the poll-accuracy table are our own averages of October polls.
  • For 2024, we have day-by-day county early-vote numbers only for counties with archived live trackers.
  • Third-party candidates are left out; all margins are between the two major candidates.
  • Two backtests are a small sample. Every election forecast faces this, because elections are rare.

Updates and checks

The site refreshes automatically: every 20 minutes from 8 a.m. to 10 p.m. Central during early voting, three times a day otherwise, and every 10 minutes on election night. Each refresh runs a set of automated tests and sanity checks (for example, a poll average that jumps unrealistically or a county count that goes down). If any check fails, nothing is published and the site keeps its last good version.
